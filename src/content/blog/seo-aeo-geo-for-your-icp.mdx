Most SEO advice starts with a keyword tool. Type in a topic, sort by volume, pick something with low difficulty, write 1,500 words. I've started wondering if that's backwards.

A keyword tool tells you what a lot of people search. It doesn't tell you which of those people would ever buy from you. You can rank, get traffic, and still have no one from your ICP (ideal customer profile) reading. And now there are two more places to show up: the answer boxes in search, and AI tools like ChatGPT and Perplexity. That's where SEO, AEO and GEO come in.

This post shows how to write one page, built around your ICP, that works for all three.

**Key takeaways**

- Start with the person, not the keyword. Collect the words your ICP uses before you open a keyword tool.
- Match each post to how far along your reader is: aware of the problem, looking at solutions, or comparing products.
- For AEO, put the question in the heading and answer it in the first two sentences.
- For GEO, be specific, quotable, and mentioned in places other than your own site.
- Measure whether the right people find you, not just how many.

## SEO vs AEO vs GEO: what's the difference?

**SEO** (search engine optimization) gets your page ranked in search results. **AEO** (answer engine optimization) gets your answer shown directly, in featured snippets, "People also ask" and voice answers. **GEO** (generative engine optimization) gets you mentioned or cited inside AI-written answers.

| | SEO | AEO | GEO |
|--|-----|-----|-----|
| Where you show up | The list of search results | Answer boxes, "People also ask", voice assistants | Answers from ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini, Claude, Google's AI results |
| What wins | A relevant, well-built page other sites link to | A short, direct answer to one clear question | Specific, quotable content that other sources also mention |
| What you get | A click | Visibility, sometimes a click | A mention or a cited link |
| How to check | Google Search Console | Search the question yourself | Ask the AI tools yourself |

The terms aren't standardized, and some people treat AEO and GEO as the same thing. In practice the work overlaps a lot. A clear page that answers a real question from someone with real experience does well in all three. Google's own guidance on [helpful, people-first content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content) points the same way.

What all three have in common is that they reward answering the right question. And the right question comes from your ICP.

## The biggest mistake: starting with the keyword, not the person

Before any keyword, write down what your ICP is trying to get done this week, what goes wrong when they try, and the exact words they use to describe it.

The last one matters most. Your ICP doesn't search with your product's vocabulary. They search with their problem's vocabulary.

Say you sell payroll software to restaurant owners. A restaurant owner rarely searches "cloud payroll platform". They search "how to pay tipped employees" or "how to do payroll for a small restaurant". Those are the words that come up when they describe their week, not when you describe your product.

Where to find those words:

- Sales call notes and recordings
- Replies to your outreach and support emails
- Reddit threads, LinkedIn posts and community forums where your ICP complains
- The "People also ask" box and autocomplete in Google
- Asking an AI tool "what questions does a [your ICP] ask about [problem]?", then checking each one against real conversations

If you have none of the first three yet, that's the first job. A handful of real conversations will give you better keywords than any tool.

In B2B, more than one person is often involved in the buying decision. The owner, the manager who'll use it, maybe an accountant. Write down the words for each of them, and decide which one this post is for.

## Match each search to where your ICP is in their problem

Not every search from your ICP is the same. Roughly, they come at three stages.

| Stage | What they search (restaurant example) | What the post should do |
|-------|----------------------------------------|-------------------------|
| Problem-aware | "how to pay tipped employees" | Name the problem, explain why it's hard, give a fix they can use today |
| Solution-aware | "restaurant payroll spreadsheet vs software" | Compare approaches honestly, including ones that don't need your product |
| Product-aware | "[product] vs [alternative]", "best payroll for restaurants" | Show exactly what you do, who it's not for, and what it costs |

Small companies tend to jump straight to "best X tools" posts. Those are crowded, and the reader is already comparing. Problem-aware posts are where a small site has a chance, and where your ICP meets you before they meet your competitors.

Now open the keyword tool. Use it to check the phrases you collected: is anyone searching them, how hard are they to rank for, and what ranks today. A phrase only your ICP searches, even rarely, is often worth more than a big one that everybody searches.

Then write several posts around one problem and link them to each other. A group of connected posts on one topic tells search engines, and readers, that you know the topic well.

## Write the post for one reader

Once you have the phrase, picture one person from your ICP reading it. Not "restaurant industry professionals". One owner, at 11 pm, after closing, trying to get payroll done before Friday.

That changes how you write.

Use their examples. A double shift, a tip pool, an employee who quit mid-week. Not a generic "business owner".

Answer the question they'll ask next. If they read about tip payouts, the next question is "what about taxes on tips?" Answer it in the post, so they don't go back to search.

Say who it's not for. "If you run a chain with an HR team, this is more than you need." Some readers will leave. The ones who stay trust you more.

Put your own experience in. Generic advice is everywhere, for search engines and AI tools alike. What they can't find anywhere else is what happened when you tried it.

The basics still apply: keyword in the title, the first paragraph, one H2 and the meta description. One page per topic. Fast, mobile-friendly pages.

## AEO: write so your answer can be lifted out

Answer engines pull a short passage out of a page and show it directly. So write passages that can be pulled out.

- **Use the question as a heading.** "How do you pay tipped employees?" as an H2, phrased the way your ICP asks it.
- **Answer in the first two sentences under it.** Answer first, explanation after. Someone who reads only those lines should still get the answer.
- **One question per section.** A section that answers three things at once is hard to lift out.
- **Use lists and tables** for steps and comparisons. Easy for people to scan, easy for machines to parse.
- **Add an FAQ section** with short answers to the questions your ICP asks around the topic. This post has one at the end.
- **Add structured data** (Article, FAQPage, HowTo where it fits). Google has cut back on showing some of these as special results, so treat it as a way to make the page clear to machines, not a trick for more clicks.

The questions have to come from your ICP. A post built around questions they never ask will win answer boxes for the wrong people.

## GEO: be the source an AI wants to cite

Your ICP is already asking AI tools things like "what's the easiest way to do payroll for a small restaurant?" The answer either mentions you or it doesn't.

This is newer than SEO, and nobody outside these companies knows exactly how sources get picked. So I'll stick to what makes sense and costs little.

- **Be specific and quotable.** Clear claims, numbers you can stand behind, named examples, short definitions. A vague paragraph gives a model nothing to quote.
- **Define things plainly.** "AEO is..." in one sentence. Short definitions are easy to quote.
- **Describe yourself the same way everywhere.** Your site, your profiles, directories. If you're described five different ways, it's harder to connect them to one person or company.
- **Get mentioned where your ICP already talks.** Reddit threads, community answers, comparison posts, podcasts, guest posts. AI answers often lean on what other people say about you, not only on your own site.
- **Publish what only you have.** Your own data, your own process, what failed. That's the part a model can't get from the ten other posts on the same topic.
- **Don't block the crawlers by accident.** AI companies run their own crawlers (GPTBot, ClaudeBot and PerplexityBot are a few). Check your [robots.txt](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/crawling-indexing/robots/intro). If you block them, you've opted out. Whether you want that is your call.
- **Consider llms.txt.** It's a [proposed file](https://llmstxt.org/) that points AI tools to your key pages. It's not a standard, and it's unclear which tools read it. Cheap to add, but don't expect much.
- **Keep it fresh.** Show a "last updated" date and update the post when things change.

## How to tell if it's working

**SEO:** In Google Search Console, look at the queries that bring impressions and clicks, and ask whether they sound like your ICP. If the top queries come from people who'd never buy, the post ranks for the wrong reason.

**AEO:** Search your ICP's questions yourself. Check whether your page shows up in the answer box or in "People also ask".

**GEO:** There's no Search Console for this yet. Once a month, ask ChatGPT, Perplexity and Gemini the five questions your ICP asks most. Note whether you're mentioned, and who is. It's manual and rough, but over a few months it shows a trend. Also watch for referral traffic from those tools in your analytics.

**For all three:** Count the right readers, not all readers. Which posts lead to a sign-up, a reply or a sales call? A post with little traffic that brings in your ICP is worth more than a popular one that doesn't.

## One page, three engines: the checklist

1. One ICP question, in their words, as the title.
2. A direct answer in the first paragraph.
3. Sections headed by the next questions they'd ask, each answered up top.
4. One concrete example from your own experience.
5. A clear line on who it's for and who it isn't.
6. Lists or tables for steps and comparisons, plus a short FAQ.
7. Links to your other posts on the same problem.

Then write the next post for the next question.

Traffic tells you how many people found you. Your ICP tells you whether the right ones did.

And those two numbers can look very different.
